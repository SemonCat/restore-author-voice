# Diagnostic lens

Use these lenses to locate loss. Do not turn them into a score.

## Identity

Compare stable habits across attributable samples: function words, transitions, punctuation, paragraph shape, terms of address, technical vocabulary, code-switching, formality, qualification, disagreement, humor, and requests for action. One occurrence is not a trait.

**Question:** Did editing push the author into a generic house style?

## Rhythm

Match information pressure to purpose. Exposition may stay plain, evidence may become dense, and a real turn may justify a pause. End on the decision, consequence, action, or unresolved fact. Do not randomize sentence length.

**Question:** Does rhythm track the reasoning, or is every sentence equally polished and emphatic?

## Semantics

Check:

- **Specificity:** verifiable people, actions, dates, places, numbers, limits, and costs.
- **Owned judgment:** choices, objections, preferences, tradeoffs, and uncertainty.
- **Presence:** observations or constraints that cannot be swapped between arbitrary authors.

First person alone is not presence.

## Genre

Specifications need precision; incident reviews need evidence, ownership, and chronology; business messages need decisions and next actions; essays can carry more author detail; legal and high-risk text must not gain manufactured personality.

## High-information questions

Ask at most three:

- What did you do, and where did the result differ from expectation?
- Which number, cost, date, or constraint changes the reader's understanding?
- Which judgment are you unwilling to neutralize?
- What would a reasonable peer dispute, and what do you still choose?
- Which detail would only someone present know?
- Which sentence sounds most like you, and which would you never say?

## Author-swap test

Replace the author with a plausible peer. If every paragraph still works unchanged, the draft may be competent but authorless. Restore only source-backed distinctions.
