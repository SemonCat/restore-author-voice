# English prose

Apply this branch when the target language is English. Preserve the author's dialect, terminology, quotations, and deliberate code-switching.

## Priorities

1. Preserve facts, stance, uncertainty, and genre.
2. Match the author's English variety and level of formality.
3. Prefer concrete verbs and nouns over editorial gloss.
4. Let rhythm follow the reasoning instead of forcing variety.

## Locale and mechanics

- Keep US, UK, Canadian, Australian, or other established spelling consistent.
- Preserve the author's quotation-mark and serial-comma conventions unless a required style guide overrides them.
- Keep dates, numbers, units, capitalization, and technical identifiers in the source convention.
- Use contractions when the author and genre support them; do not add contractions to simulate informality.
- Keep precise domain terms. Replace jargon only when a plainer phrase preserves the same meaning.

## Sentence and paragraph shape

- Use active voice when the actor matters. Keep passive voice when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or intentionally backgrounded.
- Let key evidence carry more detail. Let transitions and routine facts stay plain.
- Keep a real contrast, repeated key term, three-part set, parenthesis, colon, or dash when it fits the author's prose.
- Break a dense sentence when its logic becomes hard to follow, not because a length rule fired.
- Address the reader only when the genre treats the audience as present.

## Voice boundaries

- First person must come from the draft, explicit instruction, or attributable samples.
- Do not manufacture candor with “honestly”, “to be clear”, slang, fragments, or profanity.
- Do not flatten regional or professional English into a generic US tech-blog voice.
- When translating, carry over meaning, evidence strength, and social register. Do not copy the source language's syntax mechanically.

Natural English may be formal, quiet, repetitive, or unresolved. The target is the author writing well, not a universal conversational voice.
